LAB-DE: German Legal Agent Benchmark

Evaluating German legal reasoning with LLM agents, case-specific rubrics, and web research

Aaron Steiner, Christian Bizer
Data and Web Science Group, University of Mannheim
Code Results
Ongoing research. We are investigating how agent harnesses and web research affect German legal case solutions, and how reliably individual reasoning steps can be evaluated. This page documents our approach, initial experiments, and open questions. Legal review and further validation are in progress. Last updated September 11, 2026.

Abstract

Large language models have developed from chatbots into agents that plan their work, use tools, and produce complete documents. Private individuals and legal professionals increasingly use them to draft legal texts. LAB-DE investigates how agent workflows and access to research tools affect the quality of these drafts under German law. It is a benchmark for evaluating LLM agents on German legal case work. Each task asks for a complete written legal opinion (Gutachten), and each answer is scored against a rubric of atomic pass/fail criteria, so results can be traced to individual reasoning steps. The tasks are university exam cases at the level of the German First State Examination, written and published together with their reference solutions by law faculty, and the prescribed appraisal style (Gutachtenstil) defines the expected content of a correct solution in detail.

The current release contains 45 exam cases at First State Examination level with published reference solutions, covering public law, criminal law, and civil law in equal parts. Each case carries a rubric of atomic pass/fail criteria derived from the official solution, 1,806 criteria in total. Rubrics are generated by LLMs, calibrated against the reference solution, tested in targeted experiments with deliberately defective answers, and developed with feedback from legal professionals. Submissions are scored per criterion by a committee of two LLM judges with a third model as tiebreaker.

We compare eight configurations: three models answering in a single call, the same models using OpenCode, and Codex with and without web search. We examine where working through a task over several steps improves a solution, what web research contributes, and which legal requirements the answers satisfy.

Introduction

LLM agents used in legal work produce complete documents: opinions, briefs, contracts. Evaluating them requires checking the individual reasoning steps of a complete document. For US practice, Harvey's Legal Agent Benchmark (LAB) follows this approach. It evaluates agents on more than 1,200 realistic drafting tasks scored against expert-written rubrics of atomic pass/fail criteria, building on the earlier BigLaw Bench. LAB is now publicly released. US practice differs from German law in doctrine, language, and document conventions.

For German law, BenGER provides exam-style case tasks and doctrinal reasoning tasks. Its latest version evaluates full case solutions using ten rubric dimensions, including correctness, subsumption and legal method, with human validation of LLM judges. It also studies human and human–AI solutions. In their experiments, the models answer without web searches or external tools. LAB-DE draws on the openly licensed ZJS subset of BenGER and adds experiments with agent harnesses and web access, together with an evaluation layer at the level of individual legal requirements.

Our focus. Each LAB-DE case has explicit pass/fail criteria derived from its reference solution. Judges apply these criteria without seeing that solution and record a rationale for each verdict. Application and argumentation criteria also receive a separate legal-method verdict. This supports detailed error analysis across solver configurations.

This page documents the current benchmark and ongoing research. The pipeline, tasks and rubrics and the final individual verdicts for the six original study conditions are available on GitHub. Additional experiments and legal review are in progress. LAB-DE is the German-law track of LAB-EU, which is designed to grow into further European jurisdictions.

Tasks

Becoming a fully qualified lawyer in Germany requires passing two state examinations: the First State Examination (Erste Juristische Prüfung) at the end of university legal studies, and a second one after a two-year practical traineeship. The current LAB-DE release draws its tasks from the first level. In these exams, candidates solve a fictional case in Gutachtenstil, a rigid appraisal style that examines every claim or offence step by step, and hand in one long written legal opinion. LAB-DE uses published university exam cases on this track, each released together with a reference solution by its academic authors.

The release contains 45 cases, 15 each in public law, criminal law, and civil law. 43 cases come from the Zeitschrift für das Juristische Studium (ZJS), an open-access legal education journal, via the BenGER corpus, and two from the OpenRewi casebook on administrative law. The selected source cases carry CC BY 4.0 or CC BY-SA 4.0 licences. Attribution and source provenance are recorded per task. Availability of further materials is documented separately. Human candidates typically get two to five hours per exam. The take-home assignments are written over several weeks and expect substantially deeper analysis.

Each task consists of an instruction, the case documents (the facts plus any annexes), and the name of the Markdown file the system must produce. The reference solution and rubric are stored outside the solver workspace. Current harnesses remove identifying metadata while retaining the exam format. OpenCode supports file tools and iterative drafting, with external research prohibited by the study instructions. The Codex web condition enables external research.

Benchmark Pipeline

Each exam case is used in two ways. The reference solution is turned into a calibrated rubric of atomic pass/fail criteria, and the facts and instructions form the task that the evaluated system solves. A judge committee then scores the submission against the rubric, one criterion at a time.

Overview of the LAB-DE pipeline. The judges receive the submission and the criterion text, never the reference solution. The following sections describe each stage.

Rubric Generation

Every case is scored against a rubric of atomic, binary pass/fail criteria derived from the published reference solution. The 45 rubrics contain 1,806 criteria (23 to 68 per case). A criterion states what counts as fulfilled and what does not, and it must stand on its own: the judge that later applies it never sees the reference solution. Where the official solution marks alternative legal paths as defensible, the criterion names the acceptable alternatives explicitly.

Scoring against case-specific criteria is a design decision. It makes each expected legal requirement explicit before a submission is judged and allows a reviewer to inspect individual failures. Acceptable alternative paths can be recorded during rubric construction. Related approaches include HealthBench and Harvey’s LAB. Our research examines their application to German legal case work.

A submission therefore receives dozens of individual verdicts per case, and every failed criterion names the reasoning step it stands for. Each criterion consists of a fulfilled condition describing the required examination step, a not-fulfilled condition describing the disqualifying errors, and metadata used in analysis: the function of the step, a diagnostic criticality between one and three stars, and the position of the step in the expected line of analysis.

Rubrics are generated by an LLM pipeline (gpt-5.6-sol) in six steps:

1 Atomize

Split the reference solution into legally material statements: individual claims, definitions, subsumption steps, and results.

The reference solution of the example case “Zwei Autokäufe” yields 40 atoms, among them: a claim of F against G for the purchase price under § 433 Abs. 2 BGB requires a valid contract between F and G.

2 Generate

Three generator roles (doctrine, fact grounding, adversary) each draft candidate criteria, producing an intentionally over-complete pool.

For the example case, the three roles draft 90 candidate criteria, several of them competing variants for the same atom.

3 Prune

Merge the pool into a rubric of atomic Boolean criteria, removing duplicates and compound criteria that would make verdicts ambiguous.

The 90 candidates for the example case are merged into the 24 criteria of the final rubric.

4 Calibrate

A three-model committee judges the reference solution against every criterion. Criteria the gold solution does not pass unanimously are rewritten, split, or dropped, for at most two rounds.

In the example case, five criteria were revised after the first round and one after the second. The reference solution passes all 24.

5 Outline

Extract the solution's structure as a tree and anchor each criterion to its position in the expected line of analysis.

Criterion C-001 below is anchored to outline node I, the claim of F against G under § 433 Abs. 2 BGB.

6 Tag

Classify each criterion by function (structure, legal basis, rule statement, application, argumentation, conclusion, form) and by criticality (1–3 stars, diagnostic only).

C-001 below carries the function tag legal basis and criticality two of three stars.

Example criteria

Two criteria from the civil law case “Anfängerklausur: Zwei Autokäufe” (24 criteria), quoted verbatim from the rubric:

C-001 Anspruchsgrundlage F gegen G ★★

ERFÜLLT, wenn die Antwort den Anspruch der F gegen G auf Zahlung von 8.200 € für den VW Beetle anhand von § 433 Abs. 2 BGB prüft und eine Zahlungspflicht davon abhängig macht, dass zwischen F und G ein wirksamer Kaufvertrag besteht. NICHT ERFÜLLT, wenn sie eine sachfremde Anspruchsgrundlage wählt oder einen Kaufpreisanspruch ohne vertragliche Bindung der G annimmt.

Translation: Fulfilled if the answer examines F's claim against G for payment of 8,200 € for the VW Beetle under § 433 Abs. 2 BGB and makes the payment obligation depend on a valid purchase contract between F and G. Not fulfilled if it chooses an inapposite claim basis or assumes a purchase-price claim without a contractual commitment of G.

C-003 Handeln des A im Namen der G ★★

ERFÜLLT, wenn die Antwort trotz der Unterschrift des A mit eigenem Namen ein Handeln im Namen der G nach § 164 Abs. 1 S. 2 BGB bejaht, weil F ihn aus seiner langjährigen regelmäßigen Einkaufstätigkeit als Mitarbeiter und Einkäufer der G kannte. NICHT ERFÜLLT, wenn sie allein wegen des fehlenden Vertretungszusatzes ein Eigengeschäft des A annimmt oder die Erkennbarkeit des Handelns für G nicht anhand der Umstände begründet.

Translation: Fulfilled if the answer, although A signed with his own name, finds that A acted in G's name under § 164 Abs. 1 S. 2 BGB, because F knew him as G's employee and buyer from his long-standing, regular purchasing activity. Not fulfilled if it assumes A acted for himself solely because an agency notice was missing, or fails to justify from the circumstances that acting for G was recognizable.

Rubric Validation

A machine-generated rubric can fail in two directions. A rubric that is too lax awards criteria the submission does not earn, and a rubric that is too strict fails correct legal work, for example by insisting on one doctrinal path where the law allows several. Our validation workflow therefore includes positive calibration, targeted negative checks, and legal review.

Positive checks. During generation, criteria are calibrated against the published reference solution and refined if it fails them. This checks consistency with the source used to create the rubric.

Negative checks. Targeted experiments use deliberately defective answers: a bare conclusion without reasoning, analysis attached to the wrong person or part of the case, or a planted legal error. These checks help identify overly permissive criteria and judge rules.

Feedback from legal professionals. Legal scholars have reviewed sample materials and judged answers. Their feedback informs revisions to criteria, evidence attribution, legal presentation and the treatment of auxiliary opinions.

Failure-focused audit. Criteria with persistent failures or disputed votes are reviewed against the task and reference solution (see the error analysis below). Model-assisted review identifies candidates for legal assessment and rubric refinement.

Automated Judging

Each criterion is judged individually. The judge receives the task instructions, the submission, excerpts of the case documents the criterion refers to, and the criterion text. It never sees the reference solution. The judge must first quote verbatim evidence from the submission, then reason about it, and only then return a pass or fail verdict. The prompt instructs the judge to fail a criterion when in doubt.

For criteria tagged as application or argumentation, the same judge call also returns a separate verdict on legal method and presentation. It examines the legal premise, application to the facts, and reasoning leading to the result, using the style required by the work product. A substantively wrong argument can still be methodically complete.

Our current judge committee uses models from three providers. gpt-5.6-luna from OpenAI and DeepSeek V4 Flash assess each criterion first. If they agree, their verdict is accepted. If they disagree, Google’s Gemini 3.6 Flash casts the deciding vote.

Failed judge calls are retried once. Decisions that still lack the required votes are marked as unresolved and count as failures.

The content score of a run is the share of criteria passed, averaged case by case. The style verdicts aggregate into a separate style score over the application and argumentation criteria.

The complete judge prompt is part of the repository and can be inspected below. Placeholders in curly braces are filled per criterion at judging time.

Show the judge prompt (content and style verdict per criterion)

Illustrative prompt snapshot: prompts/evaluation/combined_content_style_criterion.txt. Current prompt variants are available in the repository. Display punctuation is adapted for readability. Other criteria use a content-only prompt.

You are evaluating one Boolean legal-rubric criterion. Return two independent verdicts
from the same answer and context:

1. `style`: whether the attempted application or argumentation uses functional legal
   reasoning style.
2. `content`: whether the criterion is substantively fulfilled.

The criterion is tagged as application or argumentation, so both verdicts are required.
They must not be copied from one another. A substantively wrong argument may be
methodically complete (`content: fail`, `style: pass`), while a correct proposition may
be merely asserted (`content: pass`, `style: fail`).

The criterion may be written in German, English, French, or another case language.
Apply it in that language and do not translate legal terms unless needed for understanding.

Decide the style verdict first and record its method checks before deciding content.
Do not revise the style verdict merely because the later content verdict passes or fails.

## Style verdict
- Do not let the content verdict determine the style verdict. The legal rule, application,
  or conclusion may be substantively wrong and still be methodically well formed.
- The same person, legal object, issue, and examination station are hard requirements.
  Do not assemble a missing examination from statements scattered elsewhere.
- Pass only when the answer itself connects (1) a criterion-specific legal standard or
  decisive premise, (2) concrete facts or competing arguments, and (3) a conclusion that
  completes this exact line of examination.
- Routine points may be concise, but rule, fact, and result merely appearing near one
  another is insufficient without an expressed inferential connection.
- A standard for a neighboring issue is not criterion-specific. A complicity standard
  does not by itself examine immediate commencement, and distinguishing completed from
  uncompleted attempt does not by itself examine whether the attempt failed.
- A later voluntariness discussion that implies continued ability does not constitute a
  methodical examination of whether an attempt failed unless the answer itself identifies
  that non-failure question, applies its standard, and reaches its conclusion there or by
  an explicit, unambiguous cross-reference.
- Do not supply a missing premise, causal link, comparison, or conclusion. Irrelevant or
  misplaced exposition does not repair a missing application.
- A bare conclusion, definition without application, list of facts, or incomplete chosen
  examination path fails. Equivalent terminology and valid alternative structure pass.

## Content verdict
- The criterion text is the only substantive standard. Apply every stated PASS and FAIL
  condition. Do not add requirements or reconstruct a missing legal step.
- Accept equivalent legal wording and a valid alternative structure unless the criterion
  requires a particular form or location.
- Evidence must concern the same person, object, claim, offence, statutory provision,
  issue, and examination station. Never transfer reasoning from another scope.
- A named fact-pattern variant, hypothetical, or subpart (for example, the base case
  versus a modification) is part of scope. If the answer expressly assigns its reasoning
  to a different named variant than the criterion, set `same_scope` to false and return
  content `fail`, unless the answer explicitly and unambiguously cross-applies that
  reasoning to the required variant. Do not fail solely because the answer omits the
  variant label when its surrounding context still unambiguously addresses the required
  variant.
- Facts or intent discussed for one offence or station do not establish the subjective
  element of a distinct preparatory offence or other station unless the answer expressly
  assigns them there or makes an unambiguous cross-reference.
- A heading, issue statement, bare result, or nearby mention does not prove that a required
  rule, application, or argument was performed.
- Evaluate the reason actually given. A materially wrong provision, doctrine, station,
  person, object, or invented material fact fails when incompatible with the criterion.
- Split the PASS side into mandatory components and return fail if any is absent. Be
  conservative. When substantive fulfilment cannot be verified, return fail.

Evaluating your own submissions

The rubrics can score any answer to a benchmark case, including answers produced outside our harness. The repository contains the complete evaluation code. After cloning it and configuring API access, a single command judges an answer file against the rubric of a task: 

env/bin/python -m evaluation.run <task-dir> <answer-file> --judge-committee configs/judge-committee-luna-deepseek-gemini-tiebreaker.json --committee-tiebreaker --style-evaluation

The command reports the verdict, the quoted evidence, and the reasoning for every criterion, together with the aggregated content and style scores. The repository README documents the configuration options.

Results

We evaluate eight system configurations in three settings:

  • Single call: the model receives the instructions and all case documents in one prompt and answers in one pass, without tools (gpt-5.6-sol, gpt-5.6-terra, deepseek-v4-pro).
  • OpenCode agent: the same three models run inside the OpenCode CLI harness in a sandboxed workspace with file tools and iterative drafting, with external research prohibited by the study instructions.
  • Codex CLI: gpt-5.6-sol as a non-interactive Codex session, once restricted to the workspace and once with live web search. In the web arm, the domain hosting the source journal is blocked and searches resembling the hidden case identity are logged.

Within the original study, the effect of using an agent varies by model: gpt-5.6-sol rises from 70.7 to 73.8, deepseek-v4-pro from 44.7 to 51.2, while gpt-5.6-terra falls from 59.7 to 56.3. The additional Codex experiments show a higher mean content score with web access (77.1 versus 70.0).

Across areas of law, public law is the strongest area for six of the eight systems and civil law the weakest for seven of eight. The area gaps within a system are moderate for the strongest systems and grow for the weaker ones, up to 14.9 points between public and civil law for single-call deepseek-v4-pro.

In the original single-call and OpenCode conditions, gpt-5.6-sol has the highest mean content score, followed by gpt-5.6-terra and deepseek-v4-pro. We use these initial comparisons to identify cases and reasoning steps for closer examination.

The run logs show what the web-search arm does with its access. Across the 45 cases, the Codex web arm issued 1,211 search queries and retrieved 421 unique pages from 38 domains. Two domains account for most retrievals: gesetze-im-internet.de, the federal statute portal (222), and the decision database of the Bundesgerichtshof (99), followed by the Bundesverfassungsgericht, EUR-Lex, and the Bundesverwaltungsgericht. The agent uses the web mainly to read statute texts and current decisions of the federal courts. The blocked domain hosting the source journal was never accessed.

Error Analysis

This section presents candidate error patterns from an exploratory model-assisted review. The initial audit selected 218 criteria with no confirmed pass, including 49 with unresolved splits in the older vote reconstruction. The now-published final verdicts provide the basis for a revised analysis. The following examples inform the legal review.

Side issues are dropped

The exploratory review flags omissions: answers solve the main claim and skip secondary claim bases such as unjust enrichment, tort, or possession claims, although many cases explicitly instruct candidates to address all raised questions, if necessary in an auxiliary opinion.

One wrong turn costs many criteria

An early doctrinal error can affect several criteria. The model review flags possible examples for legal assessment. In the civil law case “Ehe und Espresso”, reviewed answers award the spouses co-ownership of a household purchase that the prevailing view denies, and five criteria hang on that turn. In the criminal law case “Grillen mit Hindernissen”, reviewed answers apply § 315 StGB directly and skip § 315e StGB, which costs five criteria at once.

Facts are misread

Systems overlook decisive details of the facts. In the criminal law case “Die Reise ins Gericht”, reviewed answers misread the perpetrator's mental image of a witness and produce four follow-up errors from that one misreading. In the commercial law case “Bezahlen für den Porsche der anderen?”, reviewed answers miss that the decisive transaction predates the register entry, the fact that carries the accepted teleological reduction of § 15 Abs. 1 HGB.

The wrong legal standard is applied

Systems reach a defensible-looking result through the wrong test. In the state liability case “Räumungsanspruch und Mietausfallschaden”, the systems deny negligence because the officials lacked actual knowledge, missing the objective standard of § 276 BGB, and three criteria fall with it. In the money laundering case “Finder-Schwindler”, the systems affirm the predicate offences but overlook the additional requirements that the current wording of § 261 Abs. 7 StGB attaches to self-laundering.

These patterns motivate questions about omitted issues, the treatment of follow-on errors, and the connection between case facts and legal conclusions. We are examining these patterns with the final verdicts and input from legal scholars.

The review also identified potential problems in our own evaluation: overly strict criteria, valid alternative paths not covered by the rubric, and evidence attributed to the wrong part of an answer. Candidate corrections and contested decisions are documented for follow-up. Some rubric edits still await re-scoring.

Outlook

Our next studies examine how agents affect legal reasoning, what web research contributes, and how closely our judgments agree with legal experts. We also plan to compare our case-specific criteria with BenGER’s broader scoring dimensions. Further work includes comparing results for the same cases, analysing individual errors, reviewing criteria with legal scholars, and modifying case facts to investigate whether models rely on memorized solutions.

We welcome legal scholars, practitioners and AI researchers interested in reviewing criteria, alternative solutions, or agent and web-search experiments. Please contact Aaron Steiner or open an issue in the GitHub repository.
