# Methodology of the error analysis

This file describes the procedure and the decisions. Figures that result from the analysis, such as agreement and frequencies, are reported in [results.pdf](results.pdf) and are not repeated here. Steps not yet carried out are written in the future tense. Every decision is recorded with its date in a separate decision log (German).

**Overview.** The error analysis works on 30 exam cases of the benchmark study, each solved by eight system configurations and rated criterion by criterion. The error classes were developed iteratively on these 30 cases. Two AI annotators assign every content FAIL independently, and the author resolves the rows on which they disagree. Every new version of the error classes is followed by a re-annotation of all rows it can reach, by both annotators and without sight of the earlier assignment. Clarifications added while the author resolved disagreements apply only to the decided rows. The result will be validated by a legal expert.

**Data and unit of analysis.** The error analysis covers 30 university law exam cases, from beginner exams to exam preparation, 15 from public law and 15 from civil law, taken from the de-core-45 study of LAB-EU. Each case was solved by eight system configurations (arms): three models in a baseline and an agent setting, and the Sol model in the Codex command line agent, with and without web access. This gives 240 combinations of case and arm, one of which has no submitted answer. A judge committee rated every answer against a case-specific rubric, criterion by criterion, as PASS or FAIL. The unit of analysis is one content criterion of one answer. Ratings are never changed. The error classes are assigned to failed ratings, and the analysis examines whether a failure can be traced to the answer text and, if so, which kind of error it reflects. A criterion can also be marked as met although the required content is absent. Such findings answer a different question, namely how reliable the automatic rating is. They are not part of the error counts so far, and depending on the time available they may be examined in more detail and reported separately. The 30 cases comprise 3,444 content FAILs. The study also contains 15 criminal law cases with calibrated rubrics and complete ratings. They may be added later. Whether the error classes fit them unchanged would then be tested.

**Case selection.** The 30 cases are all public and civil law cases of the study. The LAB-EU project assembled the study from exam cases with a published model solution whose license permits their use, and created a calibrated rubric for each case. The set is therefore determined by availability rather than drawn at random, and the results do not necessarily carry over to other law exams.

**The error classes.** The definitions, boundaries and the order of checks are in [error-classes.pdf](error-classes.pdf) and are not repeated here. For orientation, the classes are K1 content missing, split into the four levels K1a to K1d, K2 facts misapplied, K3 rule misstated, K4 facts altered, K5 citation missing or wrong and K7 designation missing or imprecise. The values S (an error that fits no class), R (not decidable from the text) and OK (failed rating not confirmed by the text) are reported separately and never counted as errors of a class.

**Assignment rules.** Each FAIL is assigned according to the rubric criterion. If the criterion requires content that is missing, the finding is missing content (K1a to K1d) or a missing or imprecise designation (K7). If the answer reaches a different result, it is classified as a misapplication (K2) or a misstated rule (K3). This holds even when it is doubtful whether the rubric requirement is legally necessary. In such cases a separate flag records the concrete legal question. The flag belongs to a distinct dimension of rubric issues and does not change the class. A finding remains unresolved only if the text itself does not allow a decision. A FAIL that the text does not confirm is recorded as not confirmed. Annotators also state a confidence for every row. It is recorded in the data but not used in the analysis.

**Development of the error classes.** The error classes were developed inductively, step by step, on the 30 cases. To start, the author went through the first criteria personally and described each error, as worked examples for the AI annotator. In the exploratory stage that followed, Codex proposed class assignments with evidence from the answer text, and Claude gave second opinions on contested findings. From these, the author defined the classes, drawing on supervisor feedback and on published guidance for grading German law exams [Bielefeld, Trier, Frenzel 2011, Steffahn/Thye 2011]. The first five classes were extended by a class for self-contradiction, by four levels of missing content and by a class for missing or imprecise designations. The exploratory assignments only served to develop the classes. The data analysed here come from separate annotation runs in which both models assigned every content FAIL independently, and the author decided every disagreement. How the classes were revised and which content FAILs were annotated again is described in the next paragraph.

**Versions of the error classes.** Every change to the error classes is tracked. During the iterative annotation runs the rules were adjusted several times. Most adjustments followed a boundary at which the two annotators disagreed again and again. For example, a definition of what counts as a legal rule and an order of precedence among the classes were added, so that a wrong rule and a wrong application to the facts are told apart in the same way, and the boundaries between a wholly missing examination, a missing legal standard and a missing application were sharpened. The largest change concerned the four K1 levels. Their boundaries were first described in prose, and two sentences added at different times pointed in opposite directions, so the annotators could justify different levels with the same text. They were replaced by a chain of four elements, namely the subject matter of the required examination, the legal standard for it, the application of the facts to that standard, and the conclusion. The level is the first element that is missing. The chain follows the structure of the German Gutachtenstil, and it was tried out on a small set of cases before it was adopted. Each adjustment was decided by the author on concrete examples from the data. After each version, every content FAIL that the change could reach was annotated again by both annotators without seeing the earlier assignment. The three clarifications added later, while the author resolved disagreements, were not followed by a re-annotation (see Resolution of disagreements). Purely editorial changes that affect no assignment did not lead to a new annotation.

**Annotators and technical independence.** Both annotators are large language models driven by command line agents, one based on Claude and one on Codex. Model identifiers and tool versions are recorded for every run, because they are part of the method and not an implementation detail. Independence is enforced technically rather than by instruction. Each annotator works in its own directory that contains only the error classes, the case materials, the task file and its own output folder. It contains no project notes, no earlier assignments and no configuration file that could carry context, and the agents run without persistent memory. The materials are exact copies of the latest version of the benchmark repository at the time of annotation, and the criterion texts and judges' reasoning are checked against it before each run. After every run the logs of all tool calls are checked for reads outside the own directory. Neither annotator read the other's results or any project file.

**Reliability measures.** Agreement is reported as percent agreement and as Krippendorff's alpha (nominal), by area of law (results.pdf, Table 2). It is computed twice. At the class level K1a to K1d count as one class, which shows whether the annotators agree on the kind of error. With the K1 levels kept apart, it also shows whether they agree on how far the answer got. The annotators separate the levels less reliably than the classes, so figures on the levels are less certain than the class frequencies. This is itself a finding and is stated as such. Both assignments of a content FAIL are always made under the same version of the error classes.

**Resolution of disagreements.** The author decided every content FAIL on which the annotators disagreed, including those that differ only in the K1 level. For each one the author read the answer text and the reasoning of both annotators, decided on the answer text and recorded a short justification that names the rule of the error classes it rests on. In the per-case lists the two assignments appeared as A and B in random order, so the decision could be made without knowing which annotator proposed which class. Where the error classes did not settle a recurring disagreement, the author clarified them in this step, for a different solution path, for altered facts (K4) and for designations (K7). The annotators did not have these clarifications. They were applied to the content FAILs the author decided, and content FAILs on which both annotators had agreed were not reopened, except three whose class did not follow the order of checks. The author assigned them to the class that takes precedence. The decisions and their justifications are in the evidence files.

**Validation.** A legal expert will review a stratified random sample of the assignments: in a first pass one example per class and per K1 level and four boundary cases on which the annotators disagreed, to be extended to up to five examples per class once the format has been tested. For each example the expert judges whether the class fits the definitions, whether an examiner would object to the answer at this point, and how serious the error is.

**Counting.** Class frequencies count findings, one per criterion and answer, each with the class on which both annotators agree or with the author's decision. The error count counts each error in an answer once, even if several criteria fail because of it. It uses the flag "Folgefehler von", which an annotator sets when a finding fails because of another error finding of the same answer: such a finding counts only at its origin, with the origin's class. Where only one annotator set the flag, the author decided by the same rule. Findings on PASS ratings and observations outside the criteria are not part of the counts so far.

**Errors hidden by the order of checks.** Each content FAIL receives only the first class that applies, and K1 is checked first. A misstated rule or an altered fact at the same place as missing content is therefore not counted. To measure this, both annotators recorded for every content FAIL they assigned to K1 whether such an error is additionally present. results.pdf, Table 7, reports the result as a range between the findings on which both annotators see such an error and those on which at least one does.

**Limitations.** Annotation is AI-assisted, and the two annotators are language models rather than independent human coders, so their agreement measures the reproducibility of the scheme under a fixed procedure and not human inter-rater reliability. Agreement is measured on the same cases on which the error classes were refined, so it may be higher than on new cases. If the 15 criminal law cases are added, agreement on them can be measured with the error classes unchanged, which would test them on cases they were not refined on. The K1 levels are less certain than the class frequencies. K1 findings measure deviations from the rubric, not necessarily legal errors, since the rubric encodes one model solution while exams often allow several defensible approaches. The distribution across classes, and especially across the K1 levels, depends on where the class boundaries are drawn. The counts cover only the primary error at each place (see Errors hidden by the order of checks). The error counts per arm follow the order of the benchmark's pass rates. They are not an independent ranking, because they rest on the same FAIL ratings. In one public law case the task question named the wrong party as plaintiff in the runs analysed, which LAB-EU corrected later. Findings decided by the author that fail only because of this are recorded as not confirmed (OK). In one civil law case, five criteria were rated for six of the eight arms against an earlier version of the rubric, while the content FAILs were assigned against the revised version. This concerns 24 content FAILs, 15 of which were found not confirmed (OK). The findings cover public and civil law only, so whether the class distribution also holds for criminal law remains open.
